What “applied AI” means in practice

Applied artificial intelligence is neither a research field nor model development. It fits an existing language model into one specific workflow so that the workflow becomes measurably faster, cheaper or more accurate. We do not train the model: we take one that exists and build around it what makes it usable.

Most of the work therefore sits somewhere other than the model — where it has always sat in an IT project: data quality, access rights, integration, testing, operations. For an internal document search, choosing the model takes a few days; clarifying the permission rules can take weeks. That ratio rarely reverses.

This is why the same rules apply here as to an SAP rollout or a data migration: scope, milestones, acceptance criteria, a test plan, an accountable owner on the business side, a handover plan. Where those are missing, the novelty of the technology saves nothing.

What it is not about

  • Training a foundation model from scratch
  • An all-encompassing “AI strategy” with no concrete process behind it
  • A chatbot on the website because a competitor has one
  • A demo that works on hand-picked examples but not on live data

What it is about

  • Picking one painful, repetitive process and measuring it
  • The smallest working solution that improves that process
  • A decision backed by numbers on whether it goes into production
  • An operable system that your own team understands

Why most AI pilots stall

The pilot stage rarely dies for technical reasons. Far more often the demo goes well, and then nobody can say what the next step should be. I see five recurring patterns, and each one has a well-established project management answer.

1. There is nothing to measure against

If we do not know at the start how many minutes and how many pairs of hands it takes today to process a request for quotation, there will be nothing to compare with at the end. Enthusiasm is not a metric.

The PM answer: the current state is measured in the first week, and the acceptance criteria are signed off before development starts.

2. The demo data is not the live data

Everything works beautifully on hand-picked sample documents. The live archive is where the scanned contracts sit, with the poor-quality spreadsheets, the three competing naming conventions and the missing fields.

The PM answer: from day one the pilot runs on real, anonymised production data, not on a cleaned-up sample.

3. There is no method for evaluating quality

A language model gets things wrong from time to time, and it does so confidently. Without a fixed set of questions and answers to re-run after every change, every refinement stays a matter of faith. This evaluation set, or “eval set”, is the most important and most frequently skipped element of the project.

The PM answer: we assemble a set of between 50 and 200 real questions together with the business owner, and run it at every version change.

4. Security and legal questions are left until last

The data protection officer or the information security lead typically enters the picture when the system is almost finished. That is when it emerges that one category of data may not leave the country, or that permissions have to be enforced at document level.

The PM answer: these people take part in the assessment; they are not obstacles at sign-off. The data flow map is drawn before the pilot begins.

5. Nobody takes it over

After a successful pilot the system stays on the consultant’s machine. There is no operations manual, no owner for the budget, and six months later nobody dares touch it.

The PM answer: handover is not a closing event but a phase planned from day one, with documentation and a shared operating period.

How I work: four phases

Every phase is decided and priced separately. You can stop at the end of any of them, and the output stays yours. The structure matters: you do not have to buy a whole project up front when nobody yet knows whether it is worth it.

01

Assessment

2–3 weeks

We walk through the candidate processes and look for where the real repetitive burden sits. We record today’s time spent and error rate, map the data sources, and clarify the data protection constraints. The output is a ranked list of what is worth doing first, and what is not worth doing at all.

02

Pilot

6–8 weeks

The smallest working solution is built for a single process, on real data, with real users. The evaluation set is created, and we measure answer quality, runtime and cost. The output is a running system and a decision pack: a numbers-backed answer on whether it goes into production.

03

Production

8–12 weeks

The pilot turns into an operable system: integration with the existing landscape, permissions, logging, backups, monitoring and alerts. We write the operations manual and prepare the users. This is where it becomes clear that the working instructions around the process need adjusting too.

04

Handover

4–6 weeks

Your team takes over operations and I stay behind them for a while. Joint incident handling, training on how to maintain the evaluation set, naming an owner for the budget. The goal is that the system keeps working once I am no longer on the project.

Capability areas

I work in four areas regularly. In each, the question is whether the process can tolerate the machine getting it wrong, and where a human approval point has to be built in.

Document processing and internal search

Corporate knowledge usually lives scattered: in contracts, in policies, in old email threads, in project documentation. Retrieval-augmented generation, or RAG, means the model first searches our own documents for the relevant passages and composes an answer only from those. That gives two things: the answer cites a source, and the model is not guessing from memory.

The typical use is an internal question-and-answer interface for customer service, the technical team or legal. The difficulties do not begin at the model: splitting up the documents, enforcing permissions at document level and getting the source citations right consume the time.

Automated reporting and decision support

In many organisations several working days a week go into gathering data from several systems, reconciling it and interpreting it. That part automates well: the data collection and the arithmetic with conventional code, the written summary with a language model. The model does not produce the numbers, it only puts into words what lies behind them.

This division is safe precisely because the model does not calculate, so there is nothing for it to get wrong at the level of the figures. The report goes out on a schedule to wherever the team already works: email, an internal chat system or a dashboard.

Monitoring and operations

An AI system is not alive simply because it has been built. Model versions get replaced, pricing changes, the document base grows, and answer quality can degrade unnoticed. That is why it has to be measured: runtime, error rate, cost, and a re-run of the evaluation set at regular intervals.

The same goes for cost. A badly written query or a runaway retry loop can produce a serious invoice if nothing alerts on it. This is classic operations work; only the metrics are new.

Your own servers or the cloud

The argument for self-hosted execution, or “on-premise”, is data sensitivity: the data never leaves the organisation, and the cost is predictable. Open-weight models — those whose parameters can be downloaded freely and run on your own hardware — have by now reached a level sufficient for many internal tasks. In exchange they ask for hardware, operations and update discipline.

Cloud models are typically more accurate and start with no operational overhead, but they come with usage-based cost and an external data processor relationship. The two do not exclude each other: it is common for the sensitive data to stay on your own servers while the rest goes to the cloud. That decision is part of the assessment, not an assumption going in.

The evidence: my own systems, in production

In the AI consulting market it is hard to tell apart someone who has actually operated such a system from someone who only talks about them. In my case you can check: I have been running AI-based systems on my own servers for years, on a schedule, on live data, with real operational consequences.

One clarification matters: the systems listed below run on my own infrastructure and are not client references. They prove that I do not know this technology from demos, but from the place where you have to fix it on Monday morning if it broke.

Scheduled analysis system for market data

Runs on business days against a 15-item watchlist drawn from several data sources. A language model translates the technical signals into business language, and the alerts land in a chat system. This one taught me how much it matters whether a system swallows an error from an external data source or surfaces it.

Weekly market discovery agent

Discovery across five European countries, with its own dashboard and more than 170 companies on record. It runs on a weekly schedule and builds on the earlier database in every round. A good example of how a repeated agent run has to be written so that it does not overwrite what the user entered by hand.

Daily email digest

An automatic morning summary of incoming mail, with filtering rules and a human approval point. A small system, but it shows precisely how to build a feedback loop into a classification task instead of trusting the machine blindly.

Code review agent team

A weekly system that reviews from four different roles — development, testing, security and architecture — and has covered 15 codebases so far. The lesson is that several agents with narrower roles give more reliable results than a single one that claims to know everything.

On-premise document search product

A retrieval-based question-and-answer system running on my own server, which has matured into a product of its own under the name Cognexa. This gave me most of what I know about document chunking, source citation and measuring answer quality.

Applied AI handbook

A 37,000-word professional handbook in 13 chapters, written for internal use and addressed to experienced IT project managers. A full chapter covers replacing the model runtime layer: a decision based on my own measurements about whether the switch paid off. Faithfulness, meaning how closely the answer stays to the content of the source document, improved by close to twenty percent in that measurement (from 0.656 to 0.783).

The technical detail, the decision logs and the measurement results are published on the simonprojects.eu sister site. The systems presented there are the professional backing for what I offer here as a service.

Who this is for, and who it is not

An honest filter is cheaper for both of us than an abandoned project. So it is worth being clear at the outset about whether your situation suits this way of working.

Probably a good fit

  • There is a concrete, repetitive process that eats human time today
  • The process has an owner who can judge whether an answer is good
  • The data exists, even if it is untidy
  • You accept that measurement is part of the project, not an option
  • A mid-sized or growing organisation where a decision fits into two meetings

Probably not

  • The goal is the announcement, not a working system
  • There is no designated business owner, only intent from the top
  • The expectation is flawless machine decisions with no review
  • The expected return is due next quarter
  • The request is foundation model development or a research task

If something on the “Probably not” list sounds familiar, it is still worth talking. The assessment exists precisely so that we can say this out loud before anyone spends money on development.

What most people ask before the assessment

At the end of the pilot there is no presentation, but a running system and a measurement record you can use to decide whether it goes into production. If the assessment concludes that the process can be improved without AI, I will say so, and we stop there.

No. Open-weight models run on your own servers today, and for many internal tasks they are good enough. Cloud models are typically more accurate and require no operational effort. The choice comes down to data sensitivity and cost, and we settle it during the assessment, not in advance.

Running the system does not call for an AI specialist; a team at system administrator level is enough. What it does call for is a business-side owner of the process, someone who can judge whether a given answer is good or not. That person validates the evaluation set, and without them there is no measurable quality.

For most internal processes, model usage is the smaller item. The bulk of the cost sits in the assessment, the data preparation and the integration — that is, in human work. That is why I quote separately for each phase: after the assessment you already know what the pilot costs, and after the pilot what production costs.

That it is sometimes wrong is not a defect but a property of the technology. The question is whether the process can live with it. This is why every production system gets source citations, a measurable error rate, and a human approval point wherever being wrong is expensive. A process that cannot bear that is one we do not automate.

I primarily take on project management and architecture decisions, while the implementation is picked up by your own team or by a developer brought in for it. My role is to keep the decisions well founded, the delivery traceable, and to make sure the system ends up in your team’s hands at the end.
